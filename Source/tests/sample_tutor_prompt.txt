FACTS TO STAY CONSISTENT WITH
An EQ turns one range of frequencies up or down and leaves the rest alone. EQ words: boomy is too much below 100 Hz; muddy is too much 200 to 400 Hz; boxy is too much 400 to 800 Hz; nasal is too much around 1 kHz; harsh is too much 2 to 5 kHz; sharp S sounds are 5 to 8 kHz; dull is too little above 8 kHz; thin is too little 100 to 250 Hz. Cut a problem before boosting anything.
A compressor turns down whatever goes over the threshold, which evens out loud and quiet parts. Ratio sets how much it turns down. Attack is NOT a delay: the compressor begins turning down the instant the sound crosses the threshold. Attack is how quickly it reaches the full turn-down. A fast attack clamps the very start of each hit; a slow attack lets the punch of the hit through before the level has dropped. Release is how long it takes to let the level come back up afterwards. A typical vocal gets 3 to 6 dB of reduction on the loud words.
A de-esser only ever turns down. It turns down sharp S sounds between 5 and 8 kHz and goes after the compressor. To use it, switch it on and lower its threshold until the S sounds soften. A gate turns the track down between phrases to hide background noise. Saturation adds harmonics, which makes a sound feel thicker and closer.
Streaming services play music at about -14 LUFS, and true peak should stay under -1 dB. A mix that is not mastered yet should sit near -18 LUFS with peaks under -6 dB.

EXAMPLES OF GOOD ANSWERS
Question: What does attack do? Answer: The compressor starts turning the sound down the moment it goes over the threshold. Attack sets how quickly it gets to the full turn-down. A fast attack clamps the start of every hit, and a slow attack lets the punch through first. Open the Dynamics tab to try it.
Question: My guitar sounds boxy. Answer: Boxy means too much between 400 and 800 Hz. Open the EQ tab, add a bell band in that range and pull it down a few dB until the cardboard sound goes. Pressing Analyse there will also list anything Mix Brain can hear.
